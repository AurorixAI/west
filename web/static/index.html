Findings that shaped the solution

Strong perspective

A car near the camera is several times larger than one across the avenue, so pixels per second mean nothing. Every kinematic threshold is in object sizes per second: √(w·h) of a vehicle box, a person's height.

The painted road is not the whole road

The first version's road polygon missed the far carriageway and covered a pavement corner, so people waiting at the kerb became a 35 s "jaywalking". The carriageway is now traced on the frame, islands cut out, and combined with where moving vehicles actually drive.

Lanes have a measurable direction

Headings of moving vehicles per grid cell are coherent (the flow-field image), so wrong-way driving becomes a test against the cell's dominant heading, with the vehicle left out of its own vote.

The signal head faces the camera

The lit bulb's colour in a fixed region gives the phase after a 1 s majority vote. If red and green are never both seen, the red-light and stop-line rules switch themselves off.

Queues look like stopped vehicles, and like crashes

Every red phase parks a queue across all lanes, often beside a lane that still moves. On the organisers' four videos a "stopped vehicle" rule reported every such queue, so the class is off; a car pulling up sharply at the back of a queue is not a crash unless the car in front is shoved. A slow jam counts as congestion only if it lasts through a green phase.

Every video is aligned to the camera first

The zones are placed once on a reference frame. Each new video is matched to it on static scenery (buildings, poles, kerbs) and the zones follow any shift, zoom or rotation, to within 2 px in our tests. Footage from another camera is recognised as such: zone rules switch off instead of drawing zebras on bare asphalt.

Riders are not pedestrians

A courier waiting on a scooter at the light is a "person" to the detector. People on scooters and figures standing still in a traffic lane are traffic, not jaywalkers.

Problem and approach

Learned perception, rules you can read.

A detector fine-tuned on this camera and a tracker do the seeing. Everything that decides an event is a short, tested rule over complete trajectories, so every output can be traced back to a reason.

Rules, class by class

ClassFires whenSegment

Not emitted: stopped_vehicle, near_miss, illegal_turn, solid_line_crossing, fire_smoke. Under macro-F1 a class we predict but the test set lacks adds a zero to the average, so we only emit classes whose rule is specific enough to be worth that risk.

Models and data

  • YOLO11s fine-tuned by us (weights/west_yolo11s.pt) from Ultralytics YOLO11 weights, with a YOLO11x teacher (not shipped); YOLOv8s/n COCO weights for the CPU profile and as the baseline. All AGPL-3.0.
  • ByteTrack as implemented in Roboflow supervision (MIT).
  • Training data: 1,086 key frames of the organisers' four sample videos, labelled by the YOLO11x teacher (drivers behind windscreens dropped), trained on 640 px tiles at the pipeline's 1280 px scale (scripts/train_detector.py). No public dataset was used for training.
  • Evaluation only: 63 public CCTV clips from TAD, with accident times from NVIDIA's AI City 2026 Track 3 annotations (CC-BY-4.0).

Report

What we built, what worked, what did not.

A two-stage pipeline. Observe decodes, detects, tracks and reads the signal; it is the expensive, cacheable part. Interpret builds the scene, runs the rules and merges segments in seconds. Part B is a separate causal estimator that never sees a future frame. A time guard thins detection if a video falls behind its share of the 3× budget.

What worked

  • Measuring lanes from the traffic instead of drawing them.
  • Size-normalised kinematics: one set of thresholds for near and far objects.
  • Offline smoothing with boundaries refined on raw positions: stop and start times land within a frame or two, which matters at tIoU 0.7.
  • Testing every rule against its closest look-alike: queue vs stopped vehicle, joining a queue vs a crash, red-phase queue vs congestion, cyclist vs jaywalker, backpack vs dog, sparse vs dense sampling.

What the real footage changed

  • On the organisers' four full videos (18 min) the rules first reported 333 events. We drew a contact sheet for every one, looked at them, and fixed the causes, not the thresholds alone: 27 of 27 "accidents" were queues, red-light stops or cars leaving the frame; most "failures to yield" were people waiting at the far kerb. Now 113 events (failure_to_yield 216 → 56, accident 27 → 0, U-turn 20 → 7, jaywalking 52 → 41), and every true case we had found stayed.
  • The first zones were wrong: the zebra is a narrow diagonal band, the crossing continues past the island, the stop line is diagonal. All re-measured on the sample frame.
  • A false "accident": with detection thinned to one sample a second, a short speed window read 0, so a passing car "stopped dead". Speed windows now always reach the neighbouring samples.
  • The first risk model alarmed on 70% of a calm clip. Weighting by the braking needed to avoid a collision (DRAC) brought it to no alarms on the sample clip. On the organisers' four full videos it still alarmed 72 times in 18 minutes with no crash in them, so the alarm now needs a conflict about 0.6 s ahead instead of 2 s: 9 alarms. The map is monotone, so the ranking of frames (AP) is unchanged; the price is later warnings.

What did not work

  • Our first version: a hand-drawn road, per-frame pixel speeds and an "anyone on the crossing" yield rule produced 35 s and 92 s segments; its red-light rule could never fire and its risk score was 0 on every frame.
  • COCO animals: a backpack is often a "dog". Obstacles need a confident detection that is not on a person.
  • Near misses: without depth, "braked hard near another car" fires in every queue. Not emitted.

Tested on other cameras

  • 63 public CCTV clips (TAD; accident times from NVIDIA's AI City 2026 Track 3 annotations, CC-BY-4.0): 51 crashes, 12 calm clips, all from cameras we never calibrated on.
  • It found a real bug. The harness starts each video's clock after importing solution.py, and lazy model loading charged the first clip 84 s against a 22 s budget. The detectors now warm up at import.
  • It exposed a limit we chose to keep. A car moving more than its own length between detections never overlaps its last box, so the tracker loses it. Buffered IoU fixed that on TAD (Part B 0.000 → 0.096, 6 of 7 alarms right), but on the competition camera, with the fine-tuned detector, it swapped ids between neighbouring cars: a false accident, and risk 0.91 on a calm clip. We kept the plain tracker; the competition camera decides. As shipped, TAD scores 0.039 (alarms before 2 of 51 crashes, none on calm clips).
  • The accident rule (Part A) stays strict: 0 of 14 crashes on a subset, no false events. It needs both parties seen standing after contact, and a knocked-down pedestrian leaves the detector's view. Loosening it would fire on every car that stops in front of a pedestrian.

Limitations and next steps

  • Thresholds were calibrated on contact sheets of every reported event on the organisers' four videos, not on full frame-by-frame labels: the frequent classes were reviewed on samples, so some of the 56 remaining failures to yield are surely false.
  • People waiting at a kerb are ignored by the yield rule, so a pedestrian just stepping onto the zebra as a car passes is missed.
  • Every U-turn is reported as illegal: the rule cannot read signs.
  • Next: label the four sample videos frame by frame and tune every threshold against evaluate.py; confirm accident candidates with a small video classifier trained on DoTA/CCD; add a homography to metres so speeds and gaps are physical.

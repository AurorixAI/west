Every traffic event on one camera, as a time segment with a class, plus an alarm before a crash.

A COCO-pretrained detector and a tracker turn the video into trajectories. A scene model learned from the traffic itself supplies lanes, directions and the carriageway. Ten hand-written, tested rules turn trajectories into events. A causal time-to-collision model scores accident risk frame by frame.

Live demo

Upload an .mp4 up to 150 s and 500 MB. The server runs the same pipeline as the submission, on CPU. Expect roughly one to two minutes per minute of 1080p video, and about three for 4K. You get the event timeline, the risk curve, and your video played back with our tracks drawn over it. The video stays in your browser for playback; the server deletes its copy after two hours.

Findings that shaped the solution

  1. Strong perspective. A car near the camera is several times larger than one across the avenue, so a pixel-per-second speed means nothing. Every kinematic threshold is in object sizes per second (√(w·h) of the box; a person's height).
  2. The painted road is not the whole road. The first version's hand-drawn road polygon missed the far carriageway and covered a pavement corner where people wait. That produced a 35 s "jaywalking" that was just people standing on the kerb. The carriageway is now learned per video from where moving vehicles drive (flow-field image), and people count as on the road only well inside it.
  3. Lanes have a direction you can measure. Headings of moving vehicles per grid cell are coherent (the flow field arrows), so wrong-way driving becomes a test against the cell's dominant heading. The vehicle under test is left out of its own vote.
  4. The signal head faces the camera. The lit bulb's colour in a fixed ROI gives the phase. Red and green phases are long and clean after a 1 s majority vote, so red-light and stop-line rules can use them. If the signal is never seen both red and green, those rules switch off.
  5. Queues look like stopped vehicles. Every red phase parks a queue across all lanes. A stopped vehicle counts only if other traffic overtakes it. A slow-moving jam counts as congestion only if it persists through a green phase.

Models and data

  • YOLOv8s / YOLOv8n, COCO-pretrained weights by Ultralytics (AGPL-3.0), shipped in weights/.
  • ByteTrack as implemented in Roboflow supervision (MIT).
  • No external dataset was used for training; nothing is fine-tuned. The only data are the organisers' sample videos.

Report

What we built

A two-stage pipeline. Observe: decode, detect and track, and read the signal (the expensive part, cacheable). Interpret: learn the scene, run the rules, merge segments (seconds). Part B is a separate causal estimator with its own detector and tracker that never sees a future frame. A time guard thins detection if a video falls behind its share of the 3× budget. On CPU a lighter profile keeps even a GPU-less run inside the budget.

What worked

  • Learning lanes and the carriageway from the traffic instead of drawing them: robust, and no calibration.
  • Size-normalised kinematics: one set of thresholds for near and far objects.
  • Offline smoothing plus boundary refinement on raw positions: stop and start times land within a frame or two, which matters at tIoU 0.7.
  • Testing every rule against a positive case and its closest look-alike (queue vs stopped vehicle, joining a queue vs a crash, red-phase queue vs congestion, cyclist vs jaywalker, backpack vs dog): 35 tests.

What did not

  • Our first version: hand-drawn road polygon, per-frame pixel speeds, and a "pedestrian anywhere on the crossing + vehicle anywhere on the crossing" yield rule. It produced 35 s and 92 s segments that could never match at any tIoU, and its red-light rule could never fire (a missing field). Its optical-flow risk score was 0.0 on every frame.
  • COCO animals: a backpack is often a "dog". Obstacles now need a confident detection that is not on a person.
  • Near misses: without depth, "braked hard near another car" fires in every queue. Not emitted.

Limitations, stated plainly

  • Thresholds are physically motivated and tested on synthetic trajectories. They are only as good as our dev labels of the sample videos; see the per-class table from scripts/dev_eval.py.
  • Illegal U-turns: we detect U-turns and cannot read the signs, so every U-turn is reported as illegal.
  • Accidents: contact plus an abrupt stop of both parties. Hit-and-run and single-vehicle crashes are missed.

Next

  • Fine-tune the detector on frames of this camera (small, distant pedestrians are the weakest point).
  • A small video classifier (e.g. trained on DoTA or CCD) to confirm accident candidates, and a learned risk head on the TTC features.
  • A homography to metres, which would make speeds and near-miss gaps physical.
